Aim & Objectives

Man-in-the-Middle Attack on Diffie–Hellman

Aim

To demonstrate a Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) attack on the Diffie–Hellman key exchange algorithm by illustrating public-value interception, substitution, and the establishment of separate shared secrets.

Objectives

  • Understand the principles of Diffie–Hellman key agreement.
  • Demonstrate interception and substitution of exchanged public values.
  • Compare normal communication with a MITM-compromised exchange.
  • Observe how separate shared secrets and derived session keys affect message confidentiality and integrity.
  • Identify authentication methods that prevent public-value substitution.
02

Theory

Understand the exchange before exploring the attack

FOUNDATION

What is Diffie–Hellman key agreement?

Diffie–Hellman allows two parties to independently establish a common shared secret over an insecure network. The parties exchange public values, not private exponents. A session key is then derived from the shared secret using a Key Derivation Function (KDF) to enable secure communication.

Public parameters

Prime modulus p and generator g are known to everyone.

Private exponents

Alice's a, Bob's b, and Eve's e are kept secret by their owners.

Public values

A = ga mod p; B = gb mod p; E = ge mod p.

Shared secret vs session key

The shared secret is the DH output. A KDF converts it into key material for an encryption session.

Explore the communication

Why does the attack work?

Unauthenticated Diffie–Hellman does not verify who supplied a public value. An active attacker can replace public values in transit and establish separately keyed connections with each participant. Authenticated key exchange, using verified digital signatures, certificates, or an authenticated pre-shared secret, prevents this substitution.

03

Experimental Steps

Follow the complete virtual experiment

  1. Enter the public prime modulus p and generator g.
  2. Enter Alice's private exponent, Bob's private exponent, and Eve's single private exponent.
  3. Calculate Alice's and Bob's public values.
  4. Run the normal exchange to observe the matching Diffie–Hellman shared secret.
  5. Switch to MITM mode and observe Eve's interception of public values.
  6. Observe Eve's substitution of both public values with Eve's public value.
  7. Calculate the two separately established shared secrets.
  8. Observe how matching or separate session-key material can be derived from those secrets.
  9. Send Alice's original message and observe its delivery without interception.
  10. In MITM mode, observe Eve's interception, modification, and forwarding of the message.
  11. Compare Alice's original message with Bob's received message.
  12. Analyze the attack's security implications and authentication-based prevention.
